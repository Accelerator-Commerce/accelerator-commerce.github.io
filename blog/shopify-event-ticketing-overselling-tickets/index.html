Why Shopify Event Ticketing Apps Oversell Tickets (and What to Check First)

Short answer: A lot of Shopify ticketing apps don't track seat capacity as its own thing — they track it as product inventory, which Shopify shares across every sales channel and location. That shared-inventory shortcut is also how events end up oversold. Before you trust a ticketing app with a capacity-limited event, ask it directly how it enforces the cap.

A real app, a real overselling incident

This isn't a theoretical risk. A 2023 Shopify App Store review of a named event-ticketing app, Experiences: Events & Tickets, reports an event oversold by roughly 40% — meaningfully more tickets sold than seats or spots actually available. The developer's 2025 reply to that review doesn't dispute the root cause; it describes manually restocking inventory as the workaround merchants are expected to use when a listing sells out faster than the app's capacity tracking can keep up. That's a telling admission: the fix is a merchant noticing and intervening, not the app enforcing a hard stop on its own.

Why this keeps happening: capacity built on inventory

Shopify's product inventory system was built to track stock for physical goods, not seats at an event. A ticketing app that represents "10 spots left" as "10 units in stock" inherits every quirk of that system along with the convenience of not building a separate capacity engine. Two of those quirks show up directly in merchant-facing documentation and reviews:

  • Inventory is shared across locations and channels. Evey's own help content attributes overselling directly to Shopify's shared inventory spanning multiple locations — a ticket "location" can draw down the same inventory pool another sales channel is also drawing from, so two systems can both believe they're selling from a full pool right up until it's empty from both directions at once.
  • Dynamic checkout buttons can bypass the usual flow. Shop Pay's accelerated checkout and other dynamic buy buttons are built to get a shopper to checkout fast — which can skip the exact reservation or hold step a ticketing app relies on to keep its count accurate in the window between "added to cart" and "payment confirmed."
  • The app's own vendor admits it isn't bulletproof. GuestManager's documentation describes its shared-capacity mechanism in exactly those terms — not a bug report from a disgruntled user, but the vendor's own characterization of a known limitation in a competing product in the same category.

Where this actually bites merchants

Overselling isn't just an awkward headcount. For a capacity-limited class, a small venue, a supper club, or a workshop with one instructor and a fixed number of seats, a 40%-oversold event means real people show up expecting a spot that doesn't exist — a refund conversation, reputational damage, and in some cases a fire-code or venue-contract problem that has nothing to do with the software that caused it.

What to ask before you trust a ticketing app with a capacity-limited event

  • Does capacity track as its own concept, or as product inventory? Ask the vendor directly, not just the marketing page — "stock" and "capacity" sound similar but behave very differently once dynamic checkout and multi-location inventory are involved.
  • What happens during a traffic spike? A high-demand on-sale is exactly when a shared-inventory shortcut is most likely to let two near-simultaneous purchases both succeed against the last available spot.
  • Is there a hold or reservation step at checkout, or does a sale only decrement the count after payment confirms — a gap during which someone else could also be completing a purchase for the same spot?
  • What's the actual remediation path if it does oversell? "Manually restock and sort it out after the fact" is a materially different answer than a hard technical stop, and it's worth knowing which one you're getting before an event, not after.

Where a display calendar fits instead

Not every event needs ticket-level capacity enforcement in the first place. If an event is free to attend with no admission to sell or scan, a dedicated ticketing app's capacity machinery — and its overselling risk along with it — isn't something you need to take on at all. See our piece on calendar vs. ticketing apps for how to tell which category your event actually needs. Fresh Cal is built for that free, unlimited-attendance side of the spectrum: a clean, publishable schedule with no capacity claim attached, so there's nothing to oversell in the first place. For events that genuinely do need paid, capacity-limited tickets, pair a Fresh Cal listing with a ticketing app — but go in with a clear answer to how that app actually enforces its cap.

Frequently asked questions

Does Fresh Cal sell tickets or enforce capacity limits?

No — Fresh Cal is a display calendar for publishing event dates and details, with no ticket sale or capacity tracking attached. That's also exactly why it has no overselling risk: there's no capacity claim being made in the first place. For paid, capacity-limited events, pair a Fresh Cal listing with a dedicated ticketing app.

Is overselling only a risk for free event listings, or also paid ones?

It's specifically a paid-ticketing risk. A free, unlimited-attendance event listed on a display calendar has no capacity cap to breach. The risk described here is for apps that sell a fixed number of paid admissions and rely on Shopify's shared product inventory to enforce that cap.

Is this a Shopify platform bug, or an app-design choice?

It's an app-design choice built on a genuine platform characteristic. Shopify's inventory system is shared across locations and channels by design — that's expected, documented behavior for stock. The overselling risk comes from individual ticketing apps choosing to represent seat capacity as that same shared inventory, rather than building a dedicated capacity-reservation layer that doesn't inherit those sharing behaviors.
