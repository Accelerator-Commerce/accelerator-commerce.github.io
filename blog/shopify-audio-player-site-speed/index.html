Will an Audio Player Slow Down Your Shopify Store?

Short answer: It depends entirely on how the app is built, not on whether it plays audio. An audio player that loads its files only when a shopper presses play, and doesn't inject render-blocking scripts on every page, has a negligible speed footprint. One that preloads tracks sitewide or ships a heavy script bundle on every page load can genuinely hurt your store's speed score — the same as any other poorly-built app.

Why this fear is so common

There's a large and consistent body of content warning Shopify merchants that third-party apps are responsible for the majority of store slowdowns — some estimates put app scripts behind 60–80% of the render-blocking JavaScript on a typical slow Shopify storefront. Guides on auditing "which apps are slowing down your store" are a whole content genre at this point, and merchants routinely post in the Shopify Community asking how to balance adding functionality against keeping their site fast.

What's notable is that none of this content calls out audio, music, or text-to-speech apps specifically — good or bad. That leaves a real gap: if you're specifically evaluating an audio player app, there's nowhere that tells you what to actually check before installing one.

What actually makes a Shopify app slow

"App" isn't the real variable — implementation is. The apps that tank a Lighthouse score share a handful of specific habits:

  • Render-blocking scripts. A script loaded synchronously in the document head, before the page can paint, delays everything a shopper sees — regardless of what the script actually does.
  • Preloading media the shopper hasn't asked for. Downloading full audio or video files before a visitor interacts with anything adds real weight to a page's initial load, especially on mobile connections.
  • Extra requests on every single page, even pages where the app's feature isn't actually used or visible.
  • Layout shift. An element that loads late and shoves the rest of the page around hurts Core Web Vitals independently of raw load time.

An audio player app can do all four of these things badly, or none of them. The category itself isn't the risk — the specific build is.

What to check before adding an audio player

  • Does the player load its audio file on page load, or only on play? An audio file that only starts downloading when a shopper presses play adds essentially nothing to initial page weight.
  • Is the script deferred or async? A script tag that doesn't block the page from rendering won't show up as a render-blocking resource in a speed audit.
  • Does it only load on pages where it's actually used? A blog-post audio player has no reason to load its script on your product or collection pages.
  • Does the player element reserve its own space? A player that renders into a fixed-size container avoids shifting content around as it loads, which protects your Cumulative Layout Shift score.
  • Autoplay is a genuine liability — not just for accessibility, but because an autoplaying track forces the browser to start fetching and decoding audio immediately, instead of deferring that work until a shopper actually wants it.

How Listen Now approaches this

Listen Now loads its player script only where you've actually placed a player — a blog post, a product page, or wherever else you've added one — rather than injecting itself sitewide. Audio files load on play, not on page load, and playback is manual by design, which keeps the accessibility story and the performance story pointing the same direction: nothing downloads or decodes until a shopper chooses to listen.

Frequently asked questions

Should I run a speed test before and after installing any app?

Yes. A quick Lighthouse or PageSpeed Insights check before and after installing any app — audio-related or not — is the only reliable way to confirm its real-world impact on your specific theme and page setup.

Does text-to-speech add more overhead than a simple audio file?

Not meaningfully, as long as the generated audio is cached after the first playback rather than regenerated on every page view. The performance concern is the same either way: does the file load on demand, or does something try to preload it.

Is autoplay ever worth the performance cost?

Generally no — autoplay creates both an accessibility problem and an unnecessary early network request, with no corresponding benefit for most stores. Manual playback avoids both issues at once.
