Is the Background Music on Your Shopify Store Actually Legal?

Quick answer: Playing music on a storefront is a commercial, public-facing use, and most consumer music sources — a personal Spotify, Apple Music, or YouTube Music subscription, or a downloaded MP3 you bought for personal listening — aren't licensed for that. "Royalty-free" isn't the same thing as "copyright-free" either. What actually covers you is music that's either public domain, released under a license that explicitly permits commercial business use, or delivered through a service that has already cleared that licensing for you.

"Royalty-free" doesn't mean "free to use however you want"

This is the single most common misunderstanding in background-music licensing, and it trips up plenty of Shopify merchants who assume a "royalty-free" label means no further permission is needed. Royalty-free means you pay once (or nothing, for some libraries) instead of paying a royalty every time the track plays — it says nothing about whether the license actually covers commercial, public playback in a retail environment. Read the actual license terms attached to a track, not just the marketing label on the page where you found it.

Your personal streaming subscription doesn't cover your store

Spotify, Apple Music, and YouTube Music sell personal, non-commercial listening licenses. Playing music from any of these services over a storefront's speakers, or embedding a playlist as background audio on the site itself, falls outside what a personal subscription actually permits — the same way playing a personal Spotify account over a physical retail store's sound system isn't licensed use, even though nothing technically stops you from doing it. The distinction matters because "I'm already paying for it" feels like it should be enough, and it isn't.

What actually counts as licensed for commercial use

  • Public domain music. Works with an expired copyright, or that were never copyrighted, can be used freely — but confirming a specific recording is genuinely public domain (not just an old composition performed by a modern, still-copyrighted recording) takes real diligence.
  • Music released under a commercial-use license. Many royalty-free libraries do offer licenses that explicitly cover business and commercial use — the key is that the license says so in writing, not that the track was labeled "royalty-free" somewhere.
  • Public performance licensing through a rights organization. Services like Jamendo Licensing exist specifically to clear commercial background-music use for businesses, similar to how a physical retail store might pay a blanket licensing fee to play radio or streaming music legally.
  • Music bundled with a licensed background-music app. An app built specifically for storefront background music that includes its own cleared music library has already done the licensing work — that's a meaningfully different situation from sourcing tracks yourself and hoping the label on the download page was accurate.

Why this is worth taking seriously, not just theoretically

Music licensing enforcement for small online storefronts is not a common headline, but the underlying legal exposure is real and doesn't disappear just because enforcement is inconsistent. Using unlicensed music is a copyright liability regardless of store size, and the fix — sourcing music that's actually cleared for commercial use — costs little to nothing compared to the risk of building an audio experience on a legally shaky foundation. It's also simply avoidable: this isn't a case where compliance requires expensive legal review, just picking a source that's actually licensed for what you're doing with it.

Frequently asked questions

Can I play music from my personal Spotify account on my Shopify store?

No — a personal streaming subscription is licensed for individual, non-commercial listening, not for public playback tied to a business, even an online one. Use a source that explicitly permits commercial or business use instead.

Is "royalty-free" music always safe to use commercially?

Not automatically. "Royalty-free" describes the payment structure, not the scope of permitted use — always check whether the specific license explicitly covers commercial or business playback before assuming it's cleared for a storefront.

Does a small store really need to worry about this?

Copyright liability doesn't scale with store size — the legal exposure is the same whether a store does $500 or $500,000 in sales. Since properly licensed music sources are widely available and often no harder to use than unlicensed ones, there's little reason not to start with something that's actually cleared.
