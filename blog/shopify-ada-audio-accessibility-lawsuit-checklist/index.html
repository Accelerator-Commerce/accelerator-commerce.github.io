Is Your Shopify Store an ADA Lawsuit Target? Audio Accessibility Checklist

Short answer: Shopify stores make up a large share of ADA web accessibility lawsuits, and settlements commonly run from a few thousand to tens of thousands of dollars. Audio that plays automatically with sound is a specific, well-documented trigger. The fix is straightforward: audio should be visitor-controlled, not autoplaying — which is also how a player like Listen Now already behaves.

Why this matters right now

ADA web accessibility lawsuits have kept climbing every year, and Shopify stores are disproportionately represented among the targets — reporting from accessibility vendors puts Shopify's share at around a third of all such lawsuits filed against ecommerce sites. Settlements for a first claim typically range from roughly $5,000 to $75,000, before legal fees, and that's before accounting for the cost of remediation or a repeat claim. Most merchants who get a demand letter have never had a professional accessibility audit and don't know which parts of their store are the actual risk.

Where audio fits into that risk

Audio is one of the more specific, checkable items in that risk, because the rule governing it is narrow and well established. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) success criterion 1.4.2, "Audio Control," requires that if audio plays automatically for more than three seconds, there must be a way to pause, stop, or control its volume independently of the system volume. Autoplaying background music, narration, or any sound a visitor didn't start is exactly the kind of concrete, easy-to-screenshot violation that shows up in demand letters, because it can be demonstrated in seconds by anyone loading the page with a screen reader running. A storefront that plays audio a visitor didn't ask for competes directly with the screen reader they rely on to navigate at all.

The compliance checklist

  • No autoplay with sound. Nothing on the storefront should start making sound before a visitor chooses to start it — not background music, not narration, not a text-to-speech player.
  • Visible, reachable playback controls. A play/pause or stop control needs to be on screen and reachable the entire time audio is playing, not hidden behind a hover state or a settings menu.
  • Keyboard operability. Every audio control must work without a mouse or touchscreen — this is required for keyboard-only and switch-device users, and it's one of the first things an accessibility audit checks.
  • Independent volume control. Visitors need a way to mute or adjust an audio feature's volume that doesn't depend on their device's system volume.
  • Text stays available. If audio narrates written content, the text needs to remain on the page. Audio should add an option, not replace content a screen reader or search engine can already read.
  • Document what you did. Keep a simple record of when audio features were added and how they were configured — an actual accessibility audit or legal review can move much faster with this on hand, though it's not a substitute for either.

How Listen Now fits this checklist

Listen Now's player is built around manual playback — a visitor sees a player and presses play, rather than audio starting on page load. That satisfies the core of WCAG 1.4.2 by construction: there's no autoplay to control in the first place. The player exposes a visible play/pause control and volume control, both operable by keyboard, and the underlying written content stays on the page whether or not a visitor uses the audio option. None of that makes a store immune to an accessibility claim on unrelated grounds, but it removes audio specifically as a source of risk.

What this checklist doesn't cover

Audio autoplay is one specific, checkable item — it is not the whole of ADA/WCAG compliance. Image alt text, color contrast, form labels, focus order, and dozens of other criteria are commonly cited in the same demand letters. If a store has never had an accessibility review, an audio fix alone won't close the gap. Treat this checklist as one piece of a broader review, not a replacement for one.

Frequently asked questions

Does adding an audio player increase legal risk?

Adding audio increases risk only if it autoplays or lacks accessible controls. A visitor-initiated player with visible, keyboard-operable controls doesn't add the risk that autoplaying audio does — it can actually support accessibility by giving visitors another way to consume content.

Is a three-second autoplay window actually safe?

WCAG 1.4.2 sets three seconds as the threshold for when a control becomes mandatory, but the safest and simplest approach for a storefront is no autoplay at all. Relying on a three-second window adds complexity and risk for no real benefit.

Will this checklist guarantee I won't get an ADA demand letter?

No single checklist can guarantee that. This addresses one well-documented, specific risk factor. A full accessibility audit from a qualified professional is the only way to assess overall exposure.
