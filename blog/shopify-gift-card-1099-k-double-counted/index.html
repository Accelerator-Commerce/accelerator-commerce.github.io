Is Your Shopify Gift Card Revenue Being Taxed Twice? Untangling the 1099-K Confusion

Short answer: It's a genuinely confusing area with conflicting answers circulating even inside the Shopify Community itself. The core issue is that a gift card can get counted once when it's sold and again when it's redeemed for merchandise, inflating the gross amount reported on your 1099-K above what you actually earned — but your own books, not the 1099-K, are what should determine your real taxable income.

A real, unresolved question merchants keep asking

A Shopify Community thread asks directly: how are purchases made with gift cards handled in the 1099-K form? — reporting that gift card redemptions appear to be counted as income with no simple way to back them out at tax time. A separate, later thread gives a conflicting answer, stating that gift card payments are not included in the 1099-K at all. When two threads in the same forum land on opposite conclusions, that's a sign the underlying mechanic itself is genuinely confusing, not that one merchant made a mistake somewhere.

The mechanic behind the confusion

Here's the pattern that creates the appearance of double-counting: when a customer buys a $50 gift card, that transaction can show up in gross payment volume as $50 processed through Shopify Payments. Later, when that same customer redeems the card to buy a $50 product, the order itself shows $50 in sales — but if the gift card redemption is also swept into gross payment volume at that point, the same $50 effectively gets counted twice in the numbers feeding the 1099-K, even though only $50 ever actually changed hands with a new customer payment.

A standing feature request confirms it's a real pattern

This isn't just two merchants talking past each other. A formal feature request, Stop Counting Gift Cards as "Products" and Double Counting as Sales, asks Shopify directly to fix this at the reporting level — evidence that the underlying double-counting mechanic is a recognized, recurring complaint, not a one-off misunderstanding.

Why the 1099-K isn't the number to trust for your actual taxes

The 1099-K reports gross payment volume processed through a payment processor — it was never designed to be a precise statement of taxable income, for any merchant, gift-card-related or not. It's standard practice, and expected by the IRS, that a business reconciles its 1099-K gross figure against its own books to arrive at actual reportable income, backing out refunds, sales tax, and — in this case — any gift card amount that's genuinely been counted twice. Your own sales ledger, which tracks when a card was sold versus when it was redeemed, is the more reliable source for how much revenue you actually recognized, not the 1099-K's gross total.

How to reconcile it in practice

  1. Track gift card sales separately from redemptions. Your books should show a gift card sale as a liability, not revenue — and the later redemption as the point revenue is actually recognized (see our guide on gift card breakage accounting for the full mechanics of this).
  2. Compare your 1099-K gross figure against your own ledger total for the year. If the 1099-K is meaningfully higher than what your books show as actual sales revenue, gift card double-counting is a likely contributor worth investigating.
  3. Keep documentation of the gap. If you ever need to explain a discrepancy to an accountant or during an audit, having your own reconciliation — gift card sales vs. redemptions vs. the 1099-K total — on hand makes the explanation straightforward.
  4. Work with a tax professional on your specific numbers. This article explains the pattern; it isn't tax advice for your specific filing.

This is different from breakage accounting

It's worth being precise about what this covers. Our piece on gift card breakage accounting is about your own internal bookkeeping — how to record an unredeemed card as a liability rather than revenue. This article is about a separate issue: how Shopify's own payment-processor tax reporting can overstate gross volume by counting the same gift card dollar at both the sale and the redemption. Getting your internal books right (the breakage piece) is actually what makes reconciling the 1099-K gap (this piece) possible in the first place.

Frequently asked questions

Does Shopify double-count gift card sales on the 1099-K?

The Shopify Community itself has conflicting threads on this, and a standing feature request asks Shopify to fix a double-counting pattern. The safest approach is to reconcile your own books against the 1099-K rather than relying on either source alone.

Should I report the 1099-K gross figure as my taxable income?

No — the 1099-K reports gross payment volume, not net taxable income, for any merchant. Reconcile it against your own sales records before filing, and work with a tax professional on your specific numbers.

Is this the same issue as gift card breakage?

No. Breakage is about unredeemed balances that may never get spent. This is about gross payment volume potentially counting the same gift card dollar twice — once at sale, once at redemption.
