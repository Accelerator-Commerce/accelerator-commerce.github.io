Your Shopify Events Calendar Should Feed Your Google Business Profile — Here's Why (and How)

Short answer: A Shopify events calendar tells shoppers who already found your site what's coming up. It does nothing for the much larger group searching Google Maps or "near me" for something to do. Google Business Profile has a built-in events feature — Google Posts — that can put the same tour date, class, or pop-up directly in front of local searchers, but those posts expire automatically on the event date, so they only work if someone keeps them current. Mirroring your on-site calendar into Google Posts closes that gap.

Two calendars, two very different audiences

An events calendar on your storefront reaches people who are already on your site — existing customers, email subscribers, people who found you through social. Google Business Profile reaches a completely different group: people who haven't found you yet and are searching Google Search or Maps for something happening nearby. For a store with a physical location, a touring schedule, or recurring in-person events, that second audience is often the bigger opportunity, and it's one an on-site calendar alone can't reach no matter how well it's built.

What Google Posts for events actually are

Google Business Profile supports a specific post type built for exactly this: promoting "workshops, pop-up events, book signings, or community gatherings" directly on your Business Profile, where they can surface in local search and Maps results. Unlike a standard update post, an event post can carry its own start and end date — and that's also its catch. Once the event date passes, the post expires automatically and disappears from your profile. There's no "keep this calendar updated forever" setting; it only stays current if someone is actively re-posting as new events come up.

Why this becomes a maintenance problem

A merchant who sets up a Shopify events calendar for tour dates, classes, or pop-ups has already done the harder part — tracking the schedule in one place. But Google Business Profile doesn't know that calendar exists. Unless someone manually creates a matching Google Post every time a new event goes on the storefront calendar, the two go out of sync: the on-site calendar stays accurate, while the Business Profile quietly goes stale the moment the last posted event expires. An optimized, actively maintained Business Profile is credited with driving a substantial share of local discovery traffic for businesses with a physical location or local delivery area — an expired or empty events section is a visible signal to anyone checking that the business isn't paying attention.

Where this applies

  • Tour dates and pop-ups. A band or artist's storefront calendar is exactly the kind of recurring, date-specific content Google Posts is built for — each stop is a natural, individual post.
  • Classes and workshops. A recurring class schedule benefits from a Google Post for the next upcoming session, refreshed as sessions roll over, rather than one post that quietly expires and is never replaced.
  • Trunk shows and VIP shopping nights. These are exactly the kind of local, foot-traffic-driving events Google Posts is meant to surface — a one-night event with a specific date benefits the most from showing up in a "things happening near me" search in the days before it.

Keeping the two in sync

  1. Treat your storefront calendar as the source of truth. Every time an event goes on it, that's the trigger to also create a matching Google Post.
  2. Match the dates exactly. Since Google Posts expire on the event date, aligning the post's date with the calendar entry keeps both systems honest about what's actually still upcoming.
  3. Link back to the calendar listing. A Google Post can include a link — pointing it at the specific event's page on your storefront calendar gives a local searcher a direct path from Google straight to the details and, if applicable, a way to buy a ticket or RSVP.
  4. Build it into your event-creation habit. The failure mode isn't a one-time setup mistake — it's forgetting to repeat the Google Posts step every single time a new event goes up. Treating it as part of adding an event, not a separate task, is what keeps it from going stale.

Where Fresh Cal fits

Fresh Cal keeps your storefront's event schedule accurate and easy for shoppers to browse — but it doesn't publish to Google Business Profile on its own, and no event-calendar app currently does that handoff automatically. Treat your Fresh Cal calendar as the master list, and build posting the same events to your Business Profile into your routine whenever you add a tour date, class, pop-up, or private shopping night — the calendar does the organizing, you (or a quick manual step) carry it to where the local searchers actually are.

Frequently asked questions

Does Google automatically pull events from my Shopify calendar?

No. There's no native integration that syncs a Shopify events calendar app to Google Business Profile — each event currently needs its own Google Post created separately.

Why do my Google Posts about events keep disappearing?

Event-type Google Posts expire automatically once the event's date passes. That's expected behavior, not a bug — it just means the post needs to be replaced with the next upcoming event rather than left as a one-time setup.

Is this worth doing if I only run a few events a year?

Yes, if those events are the kind that benefit from local foot traffic — a trunk show, a seasonal pop-up, an in-store class. Even a handful of well-timed Google Posts can put a specific, time-sensitive event in front of nearby searchers who wouldn't otherwise think to check your website's calendar.
