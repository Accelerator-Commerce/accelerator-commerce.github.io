Is Your Shopify Gift Card Balance Checker a Target for "Gift Card Cracking" Bots?

Short answer: Yes, by design — any storefront page that lets a shopper check a gift card balance without logging in is a viable target for automated "gift card cracking" bots that guess card numbers and PINs at massive scale until they find one with value. This is a distinct attack from stolen-card resale fraud, and the fix is on the balance-check endpoint itself: randomized codes, rate limiting, and bot detection.

A different fraud pattern than "the card was already stolen"

Most gift card fraud content — including an earlier post on this site — covers what happens after a card is already compromised: a stolen code gets drained and resold before the rightful owner notices. Gift card cracking is upstream of that. Instead of already having a valid code, an attacker's bot systematically guesses combinations of card numbers and PINs against a public balance-check page, the same way a bot might brute-force a login form, until it lands on a combination that still has money on it.

How the attack actually works

The pattern has a name in the security industry — botnets like GiftGhostBot were documented pinging retailer balance-check endpoints at up to 1.7 to 4 million guesses per hour, testing sequential or predictable card-number and PIN combinations. Because gift card numbers are often issued in predictable ranges or with short, guessable PINs, a bot doesn't need to breach anything — it just needs an endpoint that will answer "valid, $47.32 remaining" or "invalid" fast enough, at high enough volume, without getting blocked.

  • The target isn't a person, it's the endpoint. A shopper-facing balance-check page or API that returns a clear valid/invalid signal is exactly what a cracking bot needs — it doesn't matter whose card it eventually finds.
  • Success looks like a normal-looking lookup. A hit doesn't trigger a chargeback or a password-reset email the way a stolen-login attempt might. The first sign is often just a balance disappearing before the actual gift recipient uses it.
  • Volume is the tell. No legitimate shopper checks a balance thousands of times a minute from the same session pattern. That volume signature is what bot-detection tooling is built to catch.

What actually mitigates it

Security guidance on gift card cracking is consistent across the industry, and none of it involves hiding the balance-check feature from real customers:

  • Randomized, non-sequential card numbers and PINs. Predictable code ranges are what make brute-forcing tractable in the first place. Random generation forces an attacker back to a true guessing game against a much larger keyspace.
  • Rate limiting on the check endpoint. Capping how many lookups a single session, IP, or device can attempt in a given window doesn't affect a real shopper checking their own card once or twice, but it makes high-volume guessing impractical.
  • Bot detection, not just CAPTCHAs. Behavioral and traffic-pattern detection catches the volume and timing signature of automated requests, which is a more durable defense than a one-time CAPTCHA a bot can be built to solve.

Why this matters for Gift Check Pro specifically

A self-serve balance checker is exactly the kind of no-login-required lookup a cracking bot needs — that's true of any app in this category, not a flaw unique to one. The point isn't to avoid offering self-serve checking; a self-serve checker is genuinely better for legitimate shoppers than forcing a support ticket for every balance question. The point is that the endpoint doing the checking needs the same rate-limiting and abuse-detection posture as any other public lookup form, and that's a security question worth asking any gift card app — including this one — rather than assuming a balance-check feature is automatically safe just because it's convenient.

Frequently asked questions

Is gift card cracking the same as someone stealing my gift card code?

No. Stolen-card fraud starts with a code the attacker already has (through phishing, photographing a physical card in a store display, or a data breach). Cracking starts with no valid code at all — the bot is guessing combinations until one happens to work.

Does adding a balance checker to my store make me a bigger target?

It makes the specific endpoint a potential target, the same way any login or coupon-code form is. It doesn't make gift cards themselves riskier to sell — the mitigations (random codes, rate limiting, bot detection) are standard practice specifically because this attack pattern is well understood.

Can a shopper tell if their card was drained by a cracking bot versus normal use?

Not definitively from the balance alone, but a card that shows a reduced or zero balance before the intended recipient has ever redeemed it is a signal worth raising with support rather than assuming a system error.
