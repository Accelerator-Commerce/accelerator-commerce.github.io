Writing a Cancellation and No-Show Policy for Your Shopify Class or Event Schedule

Short answer: Publishing when your classes or events happen doesn't protect your revenue on its own — a clear, visible cancellation window, a deposit for limited-capacity spots, and an automatic reminder are what actually cut no-shows. A schedule without a policy just tells people when to not show up.

The gap between "when" and "what happens if you don't come"

Most merchants running recurring classes, workshops, or ticketed appearances put real effort into the schedule itself — dates, times, recurring rules, timezone handling — and then leave the cancellation and no-show question as an afterthought, or don't address it publicly at all. That's a problem the moment a spot in a limited-capacity class goes to waste because someone signed up and never showed, with no consequence and no policy on record to point to.

What a real policy needs to cover

  • A cancellation window. How far in advance can someone cancel or reschedule without penalty — 24 hours, 48 hours, a week? Vague or unstated windows are the most common source of disputes.
  • What happens after that window closes. A partial refund, a credit toward a future class, or no refund at all — pick one and state it plainly rather than deciding case-by-case.
  • A no-show consequence, separate from a late cancellation. Someone who cancels with notice and someone who simply doesn't show up are different problems and can reasonably get different treatment.
  • A deposit for limited-capacity spots. Even a small deposit meaningfully changes the odds someone follows through, because there's now something at stake beyond a calendar reminder.

Why deposits and reminders actually reduce no-shows

This isn't just intuition — it's a well-documented pattern among booking and appointment apps that handle reservations for classes and services: requiring even a modest deposit at signup, combined with an automatic reminder as the date approaches, measurably cuts the number of people who register and never appear. A published schedule with neither of those in place is relying on goodwill alone to fill seats that could otherwise be given to someone who'd actually attend.

Putting this in front of customers, not just in your head

A policy only works if people can see it before they commit, not after something goes wrong. Put the cancellation window and no-show terms directly on the page where the class or event is listed — not buried in a separate terms-of-service page nobody reads before signing up. If your event listing already links out for registration or payment, restate the key terms (cancellation window, deposit amount) right next to that link.

How this pairs with your event calendar

A display calendar like Fresh Cal is built for publishing accurate dates, times, and recurring schedules — it's the visibility half of the job. It isn't a reservation or payment system, so the policy itself, and any deposit collection, needs to live wherever registration actually happens. Pairing a clear, visible policy with your calendar listing — and an automatic reminder as the date approaches — closes the gap between telling people when something happens and getting them to actually show up.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a written policy if my events are free?

It matters less for a free, unlimited-capacity event like a pop-up appearance. It matters most for limited-capacity classes or workshops, where one no-show is a seat someone else could have taken.

Can Fresh Cal collect deposits or enforce a cancellation policy?

No — Fresh Cal is a display calendar for publishing schedules, not a booking or payment system. Deposit collection and cancellation enforcement need to happen through whatever registration or payment method you already use; the calendar listing is where you communicate the policy, not where you enforce it.

What's a reasonable cancellation window to start with?

There's no universal number, but 24–48 hours before a class or event is a common baseline that gives you time to fill the spot while still being reasonable for customers with a genuine scheduling conflict.
